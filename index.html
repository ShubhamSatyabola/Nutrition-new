Introduction to Nutrition & Dietetics

Balanced diet · Nutrients · Food hygiene · Planning guides

⚖️
Balanced Diet

tap to expand

Definition
  • A diet that provides all essential nutrients in correct quantities and proportions to maintain health, growth and body functions.
Factors on which it depends
  • Age – infants, children, adults, elderly have different needs
  • Sex – males generally need more calories; females need more iron
  • Physiological state – pregnancy, lactation increase requirements
  • Activity level – sedentary vs heavy manual labour
  • Climate – cold climates need higher calorie intake
  • Health status – disease alters absorption and requirements
  • Body size and composition – larger body = higher BMR
  • Socio-economic status – affordability of food
Key components
  • Cereals & millets – 60–70% energy
  • Pulses – protein source
  • Milk & dairy – calcium, protein
  • Vegetables & fruits – vitamins, fibre
  • Fats & oils – essential fatty acids, fat-soluble vitamins
📋
Factors in Diet Planning

tap to expand

  • Nutritional adequacy – meets RDA (Recommended Dietary Allowance)
  • Food preferences & cultural habits – acceptability of foods
  • Budget & availability of foods
  • Season & regional food availability
  • Food allergies and intolerances
  • Cooking methods that preserve nutrients
  • Digestibility & satiety value
  • Meal pattern and frequency – 3 main meals + 2 snacks ideal
  • Psychological factors – appetite, colour, texture
Guides available for planning
  • ICMR Dietary Guidelines (India)
  • Food Pyramid / MyPlate (USDA)
  • Food Groups concept (5–7 groups)
  • RDA charts – ICMR 2020 updated values
  • Exchange lists – for diabetic/clinical planning

Food Hygiene, Preparation & Preservation

tap to expand

Food Hygiene
  • Personal hygiene – handwashing, clean utensils
  • Safe storage – correct temperature, airtight containers
  • Preventing cross-contamination – raw vs cooked
  • Safe water use
  • FSSAI standards (India)
Food Preparation
  • Methods: boiling, steaming, roasting, frying, pressure cooking
  • Nutrient-preserving techniques: minimum water, short cooking time
  • Avoid repeated heating
Food Preservation
  • Drying / dehydration
  • Canning and bottling
  • Refrigeration and freezing
  • Pickling (acid/salt)
  • Fermentation
  • Irradiation
  • Chemical preservatives (BHA, sodium benzoate)
💊
Macronutrients – Review

tap to expand

Carbohydrates
  • Simple (monosaccharides, disaccharides) vs Complex (starch, fibre)
  • 4 kcal/g; main energy source; spares protein
  • Fibre: soluble (lowers cholesterol) & insoluble (aids bowel movement)
Proteins
  • Complete (animal) vs Incomplete (plant) proteins
  • 4 kcal/g; 20 amino acids – 9 essential
  • Functions: growth, repair, enzymes, hormones, immunity
  • BV – Biological Value: egg=100 (reference)
Fats & Lipids
  • 9 kcal/g; saturated vs unsaturated vs trans fats
  • EFAs: linoleic (omega-6) & alpha-linolenic (omega-3)
  • Functions: fat-sol vitamins (A,D,E,K), insulation, hormones
Water
  • 60–70% of body weight; regulates temperature, transport medium
🔬
Micronutrients – Review

tap to expand

Fat-soluble Vitamins
  • Vitamin A – vision, immunity; deficiency → night blindness
  • Vitamin D – calcium absorption, bone health; deficiency → rickets
  • Vitamin E – antioxidant; protects cell membranes
  • Vitamin K – blood clotting; deficiency → bleeding disorders
Water-soluble Vitamins
  • Vitamin C – collagen, immunity; deficiency → scurvy
  • B-complex: B1 (beriberi), B2, B3 (pellagra), B6, B9 (folate – NTD), B12 (pernicious anaemia)
Major Minerals
  • Calcium – bones, teeth, muscle contraction
  • Iron – haemoglobin; deficiency → anaemia
  • Iodine – thyroid hormones; deficiency → goitre, cretinism
  • Zinc – wound healing, immunity
  • Sodium/Potassium – fluid balance, nerve function
II

Introduction to Diet Therapy

Hospital diets · Therapeutic diets for diseases

▾
🏥
Routine Hospital Diets

tap to expand

  • Regular/Normal diet – no restriction; meets all RDAs
  • Soft diet – easy-to-chew; post-surgery, elderly
  • Semi-liquid/Liquid diet – acute illness, swallowing difficulty
  • Clear liquid diet – pre/post-operative; broth, juices
  • Full liquid diet – milk, strained soups, custards
  • High fibre diet – constipation, diverticulosis
  • Low residue diet – IBD flare-ups, pre-colonoscopy
  • High calorie-high protein – malnutrition, post-burns
❤️
Cardiovascular Diseases

tap to expand

Conditions: IHD, MI, Hypertension, Heart failure
Diet principles
  • Low saturated fat (<7% calories); use MUFA/PUFA
  • Low cholesterol (<200 mg/day in high risk)
  • Low sodium (2–3 g/day) to control BP and oedema
  • High fibre (25–30 g/day) – oats, fruits, vegetables
  • Maintain ideal body weight (BMI 18.5–24.9)
  • Omega-3 fatty acids – fish, flaxseed; reduce triglycerides
  • Antioxidants – vitamins C & E; lycopene
  • Limit alcohol and caffeine
  • DASH diet for hypertension
🫁
Gastrointestinal Diseases

tap to expand

Peptic Ulcer
  • Avoid spicy, acidic, fried foods; coffee; alcohol
  • Small frequent meals; bland, easily digestible foods
  • Adequate protein for tissue repair
Diarrhoea / IBD
  • Low fibre, low fat during acute phase
  • ORS and adequate fluids – prevent dehydration
  • BRAT diet: Banana, Rice, Applesauce, Toast
Constipation
  • High fibre (30–35 g/day); fruits, whole grains
  • Increase fluid intake (8–10 glasses/day)
Liver disease / Cirrhosis
  • Low sodium; adequate protein (unless encephalopathy)
  • High calorie; restrict fat in fatty liver
🫘
Renal Disorders

tap to expand

Acute/Chronic Renal Failure
  • Low protein (0.6–0.8 g/kg/day) – reduces uraemia
  • Low potassium – avoid bananas, potatoes, oranges
  • Low phosphorus – limit dairy, nuts, legumes
  • Low sodium – controls hypertension and oedema
  • Fluid restriction based on output + 500 ml
  • High calorie from carbohydrates to prevent catabolism
Nephrotic syndrome
  • High protein to compensate urinary losses
  • Low sodium; low fat if hyperlipidaemia present
Renal calculi (kidney stones)
  • High fluid intake (3+ litres/day) – dilutes urine
  • Restrict oxalate (spinach, tea, nuts) for oxalate stones
  • Low sodium and animal protein for calcium stones
🩸
Endocrine & Metabolic Disorders

tap to expand

Diabetes Mellitus (Type 1 & 2)
  • Carbohydrate counting; 45–60% calories from complex CHO
  • Low glycaemic index foods – oats, legumes, vegetables
  • High fibre slows glucose absorption
  • Low saturated fat; avoid trans fats
  • Regular meal timing to prevent hypoglycaemia
  • Exchange lists and portion control
Obesity
  • Energy-restricted diet (500–1000 kcal deficit/day)
  • High protein, high fibre, low fat
  • Behaviour modification + physical activity
Gout (Hyperuricaemia)
  • Low purine diet – avoid organ meats, shellfish, beer
  • High fluid intake; weight management
Thyroid disorders
  • Hypothyroid – adequate iodine; weight management
  • Hyperthyroid – high calorie; avoid goitrogenic foods
🤧
Allergy, Infections & Fevers

tap to expand

Food Allergy
  • Elimination diet – identify and remove allergen
  • Common allergens: milk, eggs, nuts, wheat (gluten), shellfish
  • Coeliac disease – strict gluten-free diet
Infections & Fevers
  • Increased BMR (7% per 1°F rise in temp) → higher calorie need
  • High fluid intake to replace losses
  • High protein – 1.2–1.5 g/kg/day for tissue repair
  • Soft, easily digestible foods
  • Electrolyte replacement (sodium, potassium)
  • Vitamin C and zinc for immunity
  • TB diet: high calorie, high protein, adequate vitamins
🔪
Pre & Post-Operative Stage

tap to expand

Pre-operative
  • NPO (nil by mouth) 6–8 hours before surgery
  • Correct nutritional deficiencies beforehand
  • High protein, high vitamin C to aid wound healing
Post-operative
  • Stage 1: IV fluids/clear liquids
  • Stage 2: Full liquid → soft diet
  • Stage 3: Return to normal / therapeutic diet
  • High protein (1.5–2 g/kg) – tissue repair
  • High vitamin C & zinc – collagen synthesis
  • Iron supplementation if blood loss
📉
Deficiency Diseases, Over & Underweight

tap to expand

Malnutrition states
  • PEM – Protein Energy Malnutrition: Kwashiorkor (protein def.) vs Marasmus (energy+protein def.)
  • Micronutrient deficiencies: IDA (iron), VAD (vitamin A), IDD (iodine)
Underweight management
  • High energy, high protein diet
  • Nutrient-dense foods – nuts, milk, legumes
  • 6 small meals/day
Overweight management
  • Calorie deficit (500 kcal below TDEE)
  • Avoid refined sugars, saturated fat, processed foods
  • Increase physical activity; behavioral counselling
  • BMI >25 = overweight; >30 = obese (WHO)
III

Infant & Child Nutrition

Feeding · Weaning · Sick child · School nutrition

▾
👶
Feeding Normal Infants

tap to expand

Nutritional requirements (0–12 months)
  • Energy: ~100–120 kcal/kg/day (high due to rapid growth)
  • Protein: 1.5–2.2 g/kg/day
  • Fat: 40–50% of calories; DHA for brain development
  • Iron: needs supplementation after 6 months
  • Calcium: 200–260 mg/day (0–6m), 260–270 mg/day (6–12m)
Breastfeeding
  • WHO recommends exclusive breastfeeding for 6 months
  • Colostrum (first milk) – rich in antibodies (IgA), proteins, vitamins
  • Breast milk ideal composition changes over time
  • Advantages: immunity, bonding, cost-effective, reduces SIDS
Formula feeding (when breastfeeding not possible)
  • Iron-fortified infant formula
  • Do NOT use cow's milk <12 months (kidney load, low iron)
🍼
Feeding Premature Infants

tap to expand

Factors to consider
  • Gestational age and birth weight (VLBW <1500g; LBW <2500g)
  • Weak suck/swallow reflex – may need gavage (tube) feeding
  • Immature gut – risk of NEC (Necrotising Enterocolitis)
  • Higher metabolic rate; rapid catch-up growth needed
Nutritional requirements (higher than term infants)
  • Energy: 110–135 kcal/kg/day
  • Protein: 3–4 g/kg/day (for catch-up growth)
  • Calcium & phosphorus: higher for bone mineralisation
  • Vitamins D, A, C, E, K supplementation required
  • Iron supplementation from 2–4 weeks of age
  • Expressed breast milk is gold standard; fortification often needed
🥣
Supplementary Feeding & Weaning

tap to expand

Supplementary feeding advantages
  • Fills energy-nutrient gap as breast milk alone becomes insufficient after 6 months
  • Provides iron, zinc, B12 not adequately in breast milk after 6m
  • Develops chewing skills and food acceptance
Method of introduction
  • Start at 6 months; introduce ONE food at a time, 3–5 day gap
  • Begin with rice/dal kanji, mashed vegetables, fruit purees
  • Increase texture gradually: puree → mashed → chopped → family food
  • No added salt/sugar/honey under 1 year
Weaning – effects on mother and child
  • Mother: menstrual cycle returns, risk of pregnancy increases, breast engorgement initially
  • Child: shift from passive (milk) to active (solid) nutrition; risk of infections if done abruptly or unhygienically
🧠
Psychology of Infant & Child Feeding

tap to expand

  • Feeding is a social/emotional experience as well as nutritional
  • Responsive feeding – respond to hunger/satiety cues; avoid force feeding
  • Food neophobia – common in toddlers; offer rejected foods 8–15 times
  • Avoid using food as reward or punishment
  • Positive mealtime environment – family meals, minimal distractions
  • Parent's anxiety can transfer to child's eating behaviour
  • Media influence on food choices in older children
🤒
Feeding the Sick Child & Diseases in Childhood

tap to expand

General principles
  • Continue breastfeeding during illness
  • Small frequent meals; easy-to-digest foods
  • Adequate fluids/ORS to prevent dehydration
  • Catch-up feeding after recovery
Common conditions
  • Diarrhoea – ORS, continue feeding, zinc supplementation
  • Fever – extra 10–12% energy per °C rise; fluids
  • Coeliac disease – strict gluten-free diet for life
  • PKU (Phenylketonuria) – low phenylalanine formula
  • Type 1 Diabetes – carb counting, insulin coordination
Deficiency states in children
  • Marasmus – severe wasting; sunken eyes, skin and bones
  • Kwashiorkor – oedema, moon face, pot belly, hair discolouration
  • Vitamin A deficiency – bitot's spots, xerophthalmia
  • Rickets – bowed legs, delayed dentition; Vit D deficiency
  • Iron deficiency anaemia – pallor, poor concentration, fatigue
🧒
Pre-school Child Nutrition (1–6 yrs)

tap to expand

Nutritional needs
  • Energy: 1000–1400 kcal/day (varies by age)
  • Protein: 1.1–1.5 g/kg/day
  • Iron: 7–10 mg/day; calcium: 700–1000 mg/day
  • Vitamin A, D, C from variety of foods
Factors in diet planning
  • Small stomach capacity – small frequent meals (5–6/day)
  • Food preferences – involve child in choices
  • Texture, colour, presentation matter
  • Avoid choking hazards (whole nuts, hard raw vegetables)
Common feeding problems
  • Food jags – eating only one food repeatedly
  • Poor appetite during slower growth phase (toddler period)
  • Dental caries from sticky sweet foods
🏫
School Lunch Programme

tap to expand

Advantages
  • Ensures at least one balanced meal/day for school children
  • Improves attendance and enrolment (especially girls)
  • Reduces hunger and improves concentration and learning
  • Reduces nutritional deficiencies in vulnerable children
  • Promotes healthy food habits from early age
Need in India
  • High prevalence of malnutrition and anaemia in school children
  • Mid-Day Meal Scheme (MDMS) – launched 1995, covers >120 million children
  • Provides 450 kcal + 12g protein to primary school children
  • Reduces dropout rate, especially in rural areas
  • PM POSHAN Shakti Nirman (renamed 2021)

Nutritional Needs – Special Groups

tap to expand

Pregnant women
  • +350 kcal/day (2nd trimester); +450 kcal (3rd trimester)
  • Protein: +23 g/day above normal requirement
  • Iron: 35 mg/day (ICMR); folate: 500 μg/day (prevent NTDs)
  • Calcium: 1200 mg/day; Iodine critical for fetal brain
Lactating mothers
  • +500–600 kcal/day above normal
  • High fluid intake (2.5–3 litres); calcium 1200 mg/day
  • Protein +25 g/day; Vitamin A 950 μg RE/day
Adolescents
  • Rapid growth spurt – high energy, protein, calcium, iron needs
  • Girls: iron-rich diet for menstrual losses
  • Zinc critical for sexual maturation
Elderly (>60 years)
  • Reduced BMR – lower calorie needs
  • Adequate protein to prevent sarcopenia
  • Calcium + Vit D – prevent osteoporosis
  • High fibre – constipation prevention; B12 – absorption decreases with age
🥜
Cheap, Nutritious & Vegetarian Substitutes

tap to expand

Substitutes for non-vegetarian foods
  • Protein: pulses, legumes, soya, paneer, milk, tofu, groundnuts
  • Iron: dark leafy greens (palak, methi), bajra, jaggery, fortified foods
  • Vitamin B12: dairy, fortified cereals (challenging in pure vegan)
  • Omega-3: flaxseeds, walnuts, chia seeds
  • Zinc: pumpkin seeds, whole grains, legumes
Cheap and nutritious food selection
  • Millets (bajra, jowar, ragi) – affordable, nutrient-dense
  • Seasonal local vegetables & fruits
  • Fermented foods – increase bioavailability (idli, dosa, curd)
  • Germinated/sprouted pulses – enhance iron & vitamin C

Methods

  • Group talks – schools, PHCs, ASHA workers

Assessing Nutritional Status

tap to expand

ABCD Method
  • A – Anthropometric: Weight, height, BMI, MUAC, head circumference, WAZ/HAZ/WHZ in children
  • B – Biochemical: Haemoglobin, serum albumin, serum ferritin, vitamin levels, urine creatinine
  • C – Clinical: Physical signs – pallor, oedema, bitot's spots, glossitis, goitre, skin changes
  • D – Dietary: 24-hour recall, food frequency questionnaire (FFQ), food diary, dietary history
Community/Group level
  • Surveys – NFHS (National Family Health Survey), NNS
  • Vital statistics – infant mortality rate, under-5 mortality
  • Ecological assessment – food supply, socioeconomic data
  • WHO growth standards for children

Current Nutritional Problems in India

tap to expand

Undernutrition

  • Wasting – 19.3%; Underweight – 32.1%
  • Anaemia – 67% children (6–59m), 57% women of reproductive age
Overnutrition / Double burden
  • Rising obesity & overweight – especially urban populations
  • Diabetes, hypertension, CVD increasing – diet-related NCDs
Micronutrient deficiencies ("Hidden hunger")
  • Iron, Vitamin A, Iodine, Zinc, Vitamin D deficiencies widespread
National response

Explain Food Hygiene in detail.
Long Q Unit I
▾

Model Answer

Definition: Food hygiene refers to all conditions and measures necessary to ensure the safety and suitability of food at all stages of the food chain — from production, processing, storage, distribution to preparation and consumption.

Key Principles of Food Hygiene
  • Personal hygiene: Handwashing with soap before and after food handling; clean clothing and aprons; no handling of food when ill
  • Safe storage: Store raw and cooked foods separately; refrigerate perishables below 5°C; use airtight containers
  • Safe cooking temperatures: Cook meat/poultry to ≥75°C to kill pathogens; reheat food to ≥70°C
  • Prevent cross-contamination: Use separate chopping boards for raw and cooked foods; clean utensils between uses
  • Safe water and raw materials: Use potable water; wash fruits and vegetables thoroughly
  • Clean environment: Regular cleaning and disinfection of kitchen surfaces, equipment, and utensils
  • Pest control: Prevent entry of insects, rodents, and birds into food storage and preparation areas
Food-borne Diseases (consequence of poor hygiene)
  • Salmonellosis, Typhoid, Cholera, Hepatitis A, Staphylococcal food poisoning
  • Common symptoms: vomiting, diarrhoea, abdominal pain, fever
💡Exam Tip: Use the WHO 5 Keys to Safer Food: Keep clean · Separate raw and cooked · Cook thoroughly · Keep food at safe temperatures · Use safe water and raw materials.
2
Differentiate between Micronutrients and Macronutrients. Give sources and functions of each.
Long Q Unit I
▾

Model Answer

Macronutrients are nutrients required in large amounts to provide energy and structural support. They include Carbohydrates, Proteins, and Fats.

Micronutrients are vitamins and minerals required in small (micro) amounts but are essential for normal physiological functions.

Macronutrients — Sources & Functions
  • Carbohydrates (4 kcal/g): Sources — cereals, rice, wheat, potatoes, sugar. Function — primary energy source, spares protein, brain fuel
  • Proteins (4 kcal/g): Sources — milk, eggs, meat, fish, pulses, legumes. Function — growth and repair, enzymes, hormones, antibodies
  • Fats (9 kcal/g): Sources — oils, butter, nuts, seeds, meat. Function — energy reserve, fat-soluble vitamin transport (A, D, E, K), insulation
Micronutrients — Key Examples
  • Vitamin A: Sources — liver, eggs, carrots, green leafy vegetables. Function — vision, immunity, skin. Deficiency — Night blindness
  • Vitamin C: Sources — citrus fruits, amla, guava. Function — collagen synthesis, antioxidant. Deficiency — Scurvy
  • Iron: Sources — liver, spinach, jaggery, meat. Function — haemoglobin formation, oxygen transport. Deficiency — Anaemia
  • Calcium: Sources — milk, dairy, ragi. Function — bone/teeth formation, muscle contraction. Deficiency — Rickets, Osteoporosis
  • Iodine: Sources — iodised salt, sea fish. Function — thyroid hormone synthesis. Deficiency — Goitre, Cretinism

3
Define Nutrition and Diet. Write a short note on Balanced Diet.
Short Q Unit I
▾

Model Answer

Nutrition: The science that studies the relationship between diet and health. It is the process by which organisms take in and utilise food substances for growth, energy, and maintenance of body functions.

Diet: The total food and drink consumed by an individual over a period of time. It is the sum of all foods habitually eaten.

Balanced Diet: A diet that provides all essential nutrients — carbohydrates, proteins, fats, vitamins, minerals, and water — in the right quantities and proportions to meet the body's requirements for health, growth, and activity.

Components of a Balanced Diet (for an average adult)
  • Cereals & millets — 400–500 g/day (energy, B vitamins, fibre)
  • Pulses & legumes — 80–100 g/day (protein, iron)
  • Milk & dairy — 300–500 ml/day (calcium, protein, B12)
  • Vegetables — 300–400 g/day (vitamins, minerals, fibre)
  • Fruits — 100–150 g/day (vitamin C, antioxidants)
  • Fats & oils — 25–30 g/day (energy, EFAs)
💡Quote ICMR guidelines for India-specific recommendations to impress the examiner.
4
Define Diet Therapy. Explain the Therapeutic Diet in Diabetes Mellitus in detail.
Long Q 🔥 Most Important Unit II
▾

Model Answer

Diet Therapy: The branch of dietetics that involves the use of modified diets as a treatment modality to manage, prevent, or cure specific diseases or medical conditions. The aim is to improve health, alleviate symptoms, and correct nutritional deficiencies while accounting for the patient's disease state.

Therapeutic Diet in Diabetes Mellitus
  • Goal: Maintain near-normal blood glucose levels; prevent complications (neuropathy, retinopathy, nephropathy); achieve/maintain healthy weight
  • Calories: Based on IBW (Ideal Body Weight) — 25–30 kcal/kg/day for normal weight; 20 kcal/kg/day for obese diabetic
  • Carbohydrates (50–55%): Complex, low-GI carbohydrates only — whole grains, oats, legumes, vegetables. Avoid refined sugar, white rice, maida, fruit juices
  • Proteins (15–20%): 0.8–1 g/kg/day; lean meats, fish, pulses, low-fat dairy. Reduce if nephropathy present
  • Fats (25–30%): Low saturated fat; avoid trans fats; prefer MUFA (olive oil) and PUFA (fish, flaxseed)
  • Fibre: 25–40 g/day — slows glucose absorption; use oats, guar gum, vegetables
  • Meal pattern: 3 main meals + 2–3 snacks; regular meal timing; avoid long fasting periods (hypoglycaemia risk)
  • Exchange lists: Used to calculate carbohydrate portions systematically
  • Avoid: Sugar, sweets, sweetened beverages, alcohol, high-GI fruits (mango, banana in excess)
  • Micronutrients: Chromium, Magnesium, and Zinc improve insulin sensitivity; include whole grains and nuts
💡Remember: GI = Glycaemic Index. Low GI foods (≤55) are preferred. Cite the ADA (American Diabetes Association) or RSSDI (Indian) guidelines for marks.
5
Explain the Diet in Cardiovascular Disease (CVD).
Long Q Unit II
▾

Model Answer

Cardiovascular diseases include coronary artery disease (CAD), myocardial infarction (MI), hypertension, and congestive heart failure. Diet plays a central role in both prevention and management.

Dietary Goals in CVD
  • Reduce saturated fat: <7% of total calories; replace with MUFA (olive oil) and PUFA (fish oil). Lowers LDL cholesterol
  • Eliminate trans fats: Avoid vanaspati, partially hydrogenated oils, processed snacks
  • Limit cholesterol: <200 mg/day in high-risk patients; avoid organ meats, egg yolk (excess), full-fat dairy
  • Low sodium: 2–3 g/day for hypertension and heart failure; reduces fluid retention and BP. Avoid pickles, processed foods, papad, salt
  • High fibre: 25–35 g/day; soluble fibre (oats, psyllium husk, fruits) lowers LDL cholesterol
  • Omega-3 fatty acids: Fish (salmon, mackerel), flaxseed, walnuts — reduce triglycerides, decrease arrhythmia risk
  • Antioxidants: Vitamins C and E, lycopene (tomatoes), flavonoids — prevent oxidation of LDL
  • Weight management: Maintain BMI 18.5–24.9 to reduce cardiac load
  • DASH Diet: Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension — rich in fruits, vegetables, low-fat dairy, whole grains; reduces BP by 8–14 mmHg
  • Post-MI diet progression: Day 1 — liquid diet; Day 2–3 — soft diet 1200–1500 kcal; gradual return to normal
💡Mention the DASH Diet by name — it scores extra marks. Also mention: Mediterranean diet is cardioprotective.
6
Write short notes on Overweight and Underweight — causes, effects, and dietary management.
Short Q Unit II
▾

Model Answer

Overweight (BMI 25–29.9) / Obesity (BMI ≥30)
  • Causes: Excess calorie intake, sedentary lifestyle, hormonal disorders (hypothyroid, PCOD), genetic factors, emotional eating
  • Health effects: T2DM, hypertension, CVD, sleep apnoea, joint problems, NAFLD, certain cancers
  • Dietary management: Calorie deficit of 500–750 kcal/day; high protein (satiety), high fibre, low refined carbohydrates and fat; small frequent meals; avoid sugary drinks; behavioural counselling
Underweight (BMI <18.5)
  • Causes: Inadequate food intake, malabsorption, chronic illness, poverty, psychological disorders (anorexia nervosa)
  • Health effects: Malnutrition, anaemia, immune deficiency, stunted growth in children, amenorrhoea
  • Dietary management: High calorie (500 kcal above TDEE), high protein (1.5–2 g/kg), nutrient-dense foods — nuts, milk, eggs, avocado; 5–6 meals/day; treat underlying cause
💡Always use WHO BMI classification. Exam formula: BMI = Weight(kg) / Height(m)²
7
Explain Routine Hospital Diets with examples.
Long Q Unit II
▾

Model Answer

Routine hospital diets are standardised diets provided to patients without specific therapeutic dietary restrictions. They are planned to meet nutritional requirements while accommodating the patient's physical condition.

Types of Routine Hospital Diets
  • 1. Normal/Regular Diet: For ambulant patients with no dietary restrictions. Contains ~2000 kcal, 60–80 g protein. Includes all food groups. Example: rice, dal, sabzi, roti, milk, fruit
  • 2. Soft Diet: For post-operative patients, elderly, those with dental issues. Soft, moist, easy-to-chew foods. Avoid raw vegetables, hard meats. Example: khichdi, curd, mashed potato, steamed vegetables
  • 3. Semi-Liquid Diet: For acute illness, difficulty chewing/swallowing. Foods that are fluid or become fluid in mouth. Example: rice gruel, dal water, custard, milk-based soups
  • 4. Full Liquid Diet: For patients with chewing/swallowing difficulty, high fever, GI upset. All foods liquid at room/body temperature. Example: milk, fruit juices, strained soups, ice cream, milkshake
  • 5. Clear Liquid Diet: Post-surgery, before colonoscopy, severe nausea. Only clear fluids that leave no residue. Example: broth, water, clear fruit juice (no pulp), ORS, black tea
  • 6. High Fibre Diet: For constipation, diverticulosis, IBS. >30g fibre/day. Example: whole grains, raw vegetables, fruits, psyllium husk
  • 7. Light Diet: Transition between liquid and regular diet. Easily digestible. Low fat, no spice, no fried food

Explain the Nutritional Needs of Pre-school Children (1–6 years) in detail.
Long Q 🔥 Most Important Unit III
▾

Model Answer

The pre-school period (1–6 years) is characterised by rapid physical and cognitive development. Growth is slower than infancy but remains significant, making adequate nutrition critically important.

Nutritional Requirements (ICMR)
  • Energy: 1–3 years: 1000 kcal/day; 4–6 years: 1350 kcal/day
  • Protein: 16–20 g/day; essential for muscle development and immune function
  • Calcium: 600–800 mg/day for bone and teeth formation
  • Iron: 9–12 mg/day to prevent IDA (Iron Deficiency Anaemia); give with Vitamin C for better absorption
  • Vitamin A: 400 μg RE/day — vision, immunity; include green leafy vegetables, eggs, milk
  • Vitamin D: Sunlight exposure + fortified milk; 400–600 IU/day
  • Zinc: 5–8 mg/day — growth, wound healing, immunity
  • Water: 1–1.5 litres/day
Factors to consider in diet planning
  • Small stomach capacity — offer 5–6 small meals/day instead of 3 large ones
  • Food neophobia (fear of new foods) — introduce new foods repeatedly and patiently
  • Easy-to-eat textures — avoid choking hazards
  • Variety of colours and flavours to develop healthy food habits early
  • Avoid excess sugar, salt, processed snacks
Common Feeding Problems
  • Food jags (eating only one food for weeks)
  • Poor appetite (physiological — growth slows in toddler period)
  • Dental caries due to sticky sweet snacks

Describe Supplementary Feeding — its purpose, advantages, and method of introduction.
Long Q Unit III
▾

Model Answer

Supplementary feeding (complementary feeding) refers to the introduction of foods and liquids other than breast milk to meet the growing nutritional needs of an infant, typically from 6 months of age while breastfeeding continues.

Advantages
  • Bridges the energy-nutrient gap — breast milk alone meets only ~50% of energy needs at 6 months
  • Provides iron, zinc, Vitamin B12, and other nutrients not adequately supplied by breast milk after 6 months
  • Promotes development of chewing and swallowing skills
  • Develops food preferences and acceptance of varied tastes/textures
  • Supports physical growth and cognitive development
Method of Introduction (WHO Guidelines)
  • Start at exactly 6 months (not before 4 months)
  • Introduce ONE new food at a time; wait 3–5 days before adding another to check for allergies
  • Start with single-ingredient, iron-rich foods: rice/dal porridge, mashed vegetables, soft fruit puree
  • Texture progression: Smooth puree → Mashed → Minced → Chopped → Family foods
  • Frequency: 2–3 times/day at 6–8 months; 3–4 times/day at 9–12 months
  • No added salt, sugar, or honey under 12 months
  • Continue breastfeeding alongside complementary foods up to 2 years
💡Key terms: Complementary feeding = supplementary feeding. Weaning begins at 6 months. Avoid cow's milk as main drink before 12 months (renal solute load).

Define Weaning. Explain its methods and effects on both mother and child.
Long Q Unit III
▾

Model Answer

Weaning: The gradual process of transitioning an infant from breast milk (or formula) to other foods and eventually withdrawing breastfeeding. It is a physiological, nutritional, and psychological process.

Methods of Weaning
  • Gradual weaning (preferred): Slowly replace one breastfeed at a time with solid food/cup feeding over weeks to months. Allows infant to adjust and avoids breast engorgement in mother
  • Baby-led weaning: Allow the infant to self-feed finger foods from 6 months alongside breast milk
  • Abrupt weaning (not recommended): Sudden cessation — causes distress in infant and engorgement/mastitis in mother
  • WHO recommends breastfeeding until 2 years or beyond; weaning starts at 6 months with complementary foods
Effects on the Child
  • Positive (if gradual and nutritious): promotes independence, dietary diversity, motor skill development
  • Negative (if abrupt or poor quality food): risk of diarrhoea, malnutrition (Kwashiorkor if protein-poor weaning foods), dehydration, emotional insecurity
Effects on the Mother
  • Return of menstrual cycle and fertility (lactational amenorrhoea ends)
  • Breast engorgement and discomfort if weaning is abrupt; risk of mastitis
  • Emotional aspects — sense of loss or relief depending on circumstances
  • Reduced oxytocin; may affect mother-infant bonding if done abruptly
💡Mention LAM (Lactational Amenorrhoea Method) — breastfeeding suppresses ovulation for 6 months (if exclusive). Weaning ends this protection.

Explain Malnutrition in detail — types, causes, signs, and management.
Long Q 🔥 Most Important Unit III
▾

Model Answer

Malnutrition refers to deficiencies, excesses, or imbalances in a person's intake of energy and/or nutrients. It encompasses both undernutrition and overnutrition.

Types of Malnutrition
  • Undernutrition: PEM (Protein Energy Malnutrition), micronutrient deficiencies (hidden hunger), stunting, wasting, underweight
  • Overnutrition: Obesity, overweight, diet-related NCDs
PEM — Kwashiorkor vs Marasmus
  • Kwashiorkor: Protein deficiency with adequate calorie intake. Signs — pitting oedema (hallmark), moon face, pot belly (ascites), skin lesions (flaky paint appearance), hair discolouration (flag sign), apathy
  • Marasmus: Severe calorie and protein deficiency. Signs — severe wasting (skin and bones), sunken eyes, wizened face, no oedema, child alert but weak, baggy trousers appearance
  • Marasmic-Kwashiorkor: Features of both
Causes
  • Poverty and food insecurity
  • Early cessation of breastfeeding; poor complementary feeding practices
  • Infections (diarrhoea, measles, TB) — increase nutrient requirements and reduce absorption
  • Ignorance about nutrition
  • Large family size; gender discrimination in food distribution
Management
  • WHO 10-Step Protocol for Severe Acute Malnutrition (SAM)
  • F-75 therapeutic milk (stabilisation phase) → F-100 (rehabilitation phase)
  • Treat infections, vitamin and mineral supplementation
  • Nutrition counselling for caretakers; follow-up at community level
💡Remember the difference: Kwashiorkor = oedema present; Marasmus = no oedema, severe wasting. Mnemonic — Kwashiorkor has Kind of puffy face.

Explain Protein Energy Malnutrition (PEM) — types, causes, clinical features, and management.
Long Q 🔥 Variant Topic Unit IV
▾

Model Answer

PEM (Protein Energy Malnutrition) is a spectrum of conditions resulting from simultaneous deficiency of protein and energy, most commonly affecting children under 5 in low-income countries. It is the most common form of childhood malnutrition globally.

Types of PEM
  • Kwashiorkor: Predominant protein deficiency. Oedema, moon face, skin changes, hair discolouration, apathy, hepatomegaly. No severe wasting initially
  • Marasmus: Severe calorie + protein deficiency. Gross wasting, no oedema, child irritable, muscle wasting, loss of subcutaneous fat
  • Marasmic-Kwashiorkor: Features of both (oedema + wasting)
Causes
  • Early weaning with protein-poor diet (starchy gruels)
  • Repeated infections (diarrhoea, measles) → increased catabolism
  • Poverty, food insecurity, low maternal literacy
  • Low birth weight (LBW) — poor nutritional start
Assessment — Wellcome Classification
  • Based on % expected weight and presence of oedema
  • SAM (Severe Acute Malnutrition): MUAC <11.5 cm or W/H <-3 SD or bilateral oedema
  • MAM (Moderate Acute Malnutrition): MUAC 11.5–12.4 cm or W/H -2 to -3 SD
Management (WHO 10-step protocol)
  • Treat/prevent hypoglycaemia, hypothermia, dehydration
  • Correct electrolyte imbalance; treat infections
  • F-75 diet (stabilisation: 75 kcal/100ml) → F-100 (rehabilitation: 100 kcal/100ml)
  • RUTF (Ready-to-Use Therapeutic Food) — Plumpy Nut
  • Provide sensory stimulation and emotional support
  • Prepare for discharge; follow-up at community level
💡PEM is both a Unit III and Unit IV topic — it can appear in either unit's question. Know both clinical (Unit III) and community prevention (Unit IV) angles.

Explain the Nutritional Needs of Pregnant and Lactating Women.
Long Q 🔥 Most Important Unit IV
▾

Model Answer

Nutritional Needs During Pregnancy (ICMR 2020)
  • Energy: +350 kcal/day (2nd trimester); +450 kcal/day (3rd trimester) above normal requirement
  • Protein: +23 g/day above normal (for foetal growth, placenta, amniotic fluid, breast tissue)
  • Iron: 35 mg/day — to meet increased maternal blood volume + foetal iron stores (IFA supplementation under National Programme)
  • Folic acid: 500 μg/day — critical in first trimester to prevent Neural Tube Defects (spina bifida, anencephaly)
  • Calcium: 1200 mg/day — for foetal skeletal development; prevents maternal bone loss
  • Iodine: 220 μg/day — essential for foetal brain and thyroid development; use iodised salt
  • Vitamin D: 600 IU/day — aids calcium absorption
  • Water: 2.5–3 litres/day
  • Avoid: Alcohol, raw meat, unpasteurised dairy, excess vitamin A (teratogenic), caffeine >200 mg/day
Nutritional Needs During Lactation (ICMR 2020)
  • Energy: +500 kcal/day above normal (to produce ~800 ml breast milk/day)
  • Protein: +25 g/day; ensures adequate protein in breast milk
  • Calcium: 1200 mg/day; prevents osteoporosis in mother
  • Vitamin A: 950 μg RE/day (colostrum rich in Vit A — protects newborn from infections)
  • Fluid: 3 litres/day — maintains milk volume
  • Iron: Requirements reduce post-delivery (no menstruation), but continue IFA for 6 months postpartum
  • Avoid: Smoking, alcohol, excess caffeine (passes into breast milk)
💡Key mnemonic for pregnancy nutrients: FIFIC — Folate, Iron, Iodine, Calcium. These are the 4 most commonly deficient and most commonly asked about.

Write the Methods of Assessing Nutritional Status of an Individual, Group, and Community.
Long Q 🔥 Most Important Unit IV
▾

Model Answer

Nutritional status is assessed using the ABCD method — Anthropometric, Biochemical, Clinical, and Dietary assessment. Additional community-level methods are used for group and population assessment.

A — Anthropometric Assessment
  • Weight, height, BMI — most widely used; non-invasive
  • MUAC (Mid-Upper Arm Circumference) — for SAM/MAM in children; <11.5 cm = SAM
  • Skinfold thickness (triceps, biceps) — estimates body fat
  • Head circumference, chest circumference — infants
  • Z-scores: WAZ (weight-for-age), HAZ (height-for-age = stunting), WHZ (weight-for-height = wasting)
B — Biochemical Assessment
  • Haemoglobin — IDA screening (normal: men >13 g/dL; women >12 g/dL)
  • Serum albumin — protein status (<3.5 g/dL = hypoalbuminaemia)
  • Serum ferritin — iron stores
  • Serum retinol — vitamin A status
  • Urine iodine — iodine sufficiency
C — Clinical Assessment
  • Pallor (conjunctiva, nail beds) — anaemia
  • Bitot's spots, xerophthalmia — Vitamin A deficiency
  • Oedema — Kwashiorkor, hypoalbuminaemia
  • Goitre — iodine deficiency
  • Glossitis, angular stomatitis — B-complex deficiency
  • Bow legs, rickets — Vitamin D deficiency
D — Dietary Assessment
  • 24-hour dietary recall — quick, easy, most common
  • Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ) — habitual diet over weeks/months
  • Food diary/record — prospective; 3–7 day recording
  • Dietary history — in-depth past dietary pattern
Community/Group Level Methods
  • National surveys — NFHS (National Family Health Survey), NNS (National Nutrition Survey)
  • Vital statistics — IMR, U5MR, MMR as indirect nutritional indicators
  • Ecological assessment — agricultural production data, food supply statistics
💡ABCD = most important 4-letter word in this subject. Use these exact headings in your answer for a structured, high-scoring response.
