Introduction to Nutrition & Dietetics

Balanced diet · Nutrients · Food hygiene · Planning guides

⚖️
Balanced Diet

tap to expand

Definition
  • A diet that provides all essential nutrients in correct quantities and proportions to maintain health, growth and body functions.
Factors on which it depends
  • Age – infants, children, adults, elderly have different needs
  • Sex – males generally need more calories; females need more iron
  • Physiological state – pregnancy, lactation increase requirements
  • Activity level – sedentary vs heavy manual labour
  • Climate – cold climates need higher calorie intake
  • Health status – disease alters absorption and requirements
  • Body size and composition – larger body = higher BMR
  • Socio-economic status – affordability of food
Key components
  • Cereals & millets – 60–70% energy
  • Pulses – protein source
  • Milk & dairy – calcium, protein
  • Vegetables & fruits – vitamins, fibre
  • Fats & oils – essential fatty acids, fat-soluble vitamins
📋
Factors in Diet Planning

tap to expand

  • Nutritional adequacy – meets RDA (Recommended Dietary Allowance)
  • Food preferences & cultural habits – acceptability of foods
  • Budget & availability of foods
  • Season & regional food availability
  • Food allergies and intolerances
  • Cooking methods that preserve nutrients
  • Digestibility & satiety value
  • Meal pattern and frequency – 3 main meals + 2 snacks ideal
  • Psychological factors – appetite, colour, texture
Guides available for planning
  • ICMR Dietary Guidelines (India)
  • Food Pyramid / MyPlate (USDA)
  • Food Groups concept (5–7 groups)
  • RDA charts – ICMR 2020 updated values
  • Exchange lists – for diabetic/clinical planning
🦠
Food Hygiene, Preparation & Preservation

tap to expand

Food Hygiene
  • Personal hygiene – handwashing, clean utensils
  • Safe storage – correct temperature, airtight containers
  • Preventing cross-contamination – raw vs cooked
  • Safe water use
  • FSSAI standards (India)
Food Preparation
  • Methods: boiling, steaming, roasting, frying, pressure cooking
  • Nutrient-preserving techniques: minimum water, short cooking time
  • Avoid repeated heating
Food Preservation
  • Drying / dehydration
  • Canning and bottling
  • Refrigeration and freezing
  • Pickling (acid/salt)
  • Fermentation
  • Irradiation
  • Chemical preservatives (BHA, sodium benzoate)
💊
Macronutrients – Review

tap to expand

Carbohydrates
  • Simple (monosaccharides, disaccharides) vs Complex (starch, fibre)
  • 4 kcal/g; main energy source; spares protein
  • Fibre: soluble (lowers cholesterol) & insoluble (aids bowel movement)
Proteins
  • Complete (animal) vs Incomplete (plant) proteins
  • 4 kcal/g; 20 amino acids – 9 essential
  • Functions: growth, repair, enzymes, hormones, immunity
  • BV – Biological Value: egg=100 (reference)
Fats & Lipids
  • 9 kcal/g; saturated vs unsaturated vs trans fats
  • EFAs: linoleic (omega-6) & alpha-linolenic (omega-3)
  • Functions: fat-sol vitamins (A,D,E,K), insulation, hormones
Water
  • 60–70% of body weight; regulates temperature, transport medium
🔬
Micronutrients – Review

tap to expand

Fat-soluble Vitamins
  • Vitamin A – vision, immunity; deficiency → night blindness
  • Vitamin D – calcium absorption, bone health; deficiency → rickets
  • Vitamin E – antioxidant; protects cell membranes
  • Vitamin K – blood clotting; deficiency → bleeding disorders
Water-soluble Vitamins
  • Vitamin C – collagen, immunity; deficiency → scurvy
  • B-complex: B1 (beriberi), B2, B3 (pellagra), B6, B9 (folate – NTD), B12 (pernicious anaemia)
Major Minerals
  • Calcium – bones, teeth, muscle contraction
  • Iron – haemoglobin; deficiency → anaemia
  • Iodine – thyroid hormones; deficiency → goitre, cretinism
  • Zinc – wound healing, immunity
  • Sodium/Potassium – fluid balance, nerve function
II

Introduction to Diet Therapy

Hospital diets · Therapeutic diets for diseases

▾
🏥
Routine Hospital Diets

tap to expand

  • Regular/Normal diet – no restriction; meets all RDAs
  • Soft diet – easy-to-chew; post-surgery, elderly
  • Semi-liquid/Liquid diet – acute illness, swallowing difficulty
  • Clear liquid diet – pre/post-operative; broth, juices
  • Full liquid diet – milk, strained soups, custards
  • High fibre diet – constipation, diverticulosis
  • Low residue diet – IBD flare-ups, pre-colonoscopy
  • High calorie-high protein – malnutrition, post-burns
❤️
Cardiovascular Diseases

tap to expand

Conditions: IHD, MI, Hypertension, Heart failure
Diet principles
  • Low saturated fat (<7% calories); use MUFA/PUFA
  • Low cholesterol (<200 mg/day in high risk)
  • Low sodium (2–3 g/day) to control BP and oedema
  • High fibre (25–30 g/day) – oats, fruits, vegetables
  • Maintain ideal body weight (BMI 18.5–24.9)
  • Omega-3 fatty acids – fish, flaxseed; reduce triglycerides
  • Antioxidants – vitamins C & E; lycopene
  • Limit alcohol and caffeine
  • DASH diet for hypertension
🫁
Gastrointestinal Diseases

tap to expand

Peptic Ulcer
  • Avoid spicy, acidic, fried foods; coffee; alcohol
  • Small frequent meals; bland, easily digestible foods
  • Adequate protein for tissue repair
Diarrhoea / IBD
  • Low fibre, low fat during acute phase
  • ORS and adequate fluids – prevent dehydration
  • BRAT diet: Banana, Rice, Applesauce, Toast
Constipation
  • High fibre (30–35 g/day); fruits, whole grains
  • Increase fluid intake (8–10 glasses/day)
Liver disease / Cirrhosis
  • Low sodium; adequate protein (unless encephalopathy)
  • High calorie; restrict fat in fatty liver
🫘
Renal Disorders

tap to expand

Acute/Chronic Renal Failure
  • Low protein (0.6–0.8 g/kg/day) – reduces uraemia
  • Low potassium – avoid bananas, potatoes, oranges
  • Low phosphorus – limit dairy, nuts, legumes
  • Low sodium – controls hypertension and oedema
  • Fluid restriction based on output + 500 ml
  • High calorie from carbohydrates to prevent catabolism
Nephrotic syndrome
  • High protein to compensate urinary losses
  • Low sodium; low fat if hyperlipidaemia present
Renal calculi (kidney stones)
  • High fluid intake (3+ litres/day) – dilutes urine
  • Restrict oxalate (spinach, tea, nuts) for oxalate stones
  • Low sodium and animal protein for calcium stones
🩸
Endocrine & Metabolic Disorders

tap to expand

Diabetes Mellitus (Type 1 & 2)
  • Carbohydrate counting; 45–60% calories from complex CHO
  • Low glycaemic index foods – oats, legumes, vegetables
  • High fibre slows glucose absorption
  • Low saturated fat; avoid trans fats
  • Regular meal timing to prevent hypoglycaemia
  • Exchange lists and portion control
Obesity
  • Energy-restricted diet (500–1000 kcal deficit/day)
  • High protein, high fibre, low fat
  • Behaviour modification + physical activity
Gout (Hyperuricaemia)
  • Low purine diet – avoid organ meats, shellfish, beer
  • High fluid intake; weight management
Thyroid disorders
  • Hypothyroid – adequate iodine; weight management
  • Hyperthyroid – high calorie; avoid goitrogenic foods
🤧
Allergy, Infections & Fevers

tap to expand

Food Allergy
  • Elimination diet – identify and remove allergen
  • Common allergens: milk, eggs, nuts, wheat (gluten), shellfish
  • Coeliac disease – strict gluten-free diet
Infections & Fevers
  • Increased BMR (7% per 1°F rise in temp) → higher calorie need
  • High fluid intake to replace losses
  • High protein – 1.2–1.5 g/kg/day for tissue repair
  • Soft, easily digestible foods
  • Electrolyte replacement (sodium, potassium)
  • Vitamin C and zinc for immunity
  • TB diet: high calorie, high protein, adequate vitamins
🔪
Pre & Post-Operative Stage

tap to expand

Pre-operative
  • NPO (nil by mouth) 6–8 hours before surgery
  • Correct nutritional deficiencies beforehand
  • High protein, high vitamin C to aid wound healing
Post-operative
  • Stage 1: IV fluids/clear liquids
  • Stage 2: Full liquid → soft diet
  • Stage 3: Return to normal / therapeutic diet
  • High protein (1.5–2 g/kg) – tissue repair
  • High vitamin C & zinc – collagen synthesis
  • Iron supplementation if blood loss
📉
Deficiency Diseases, Over & Underweight

tap to expand

Malnutrition states
  • PEM – Protein Energy Malnutrition: Kwashiorkor (protein def.) vs Marasmus (energy+protein def.)
  • Micronutrient deficiencies: IDA (iron), VAD (vitamin A), IDD (iodine)
Underweight management
  • High energy, high protein diet
  • Nutrient-dense foods – nuts, milk, legumes
  • 6 small meals/day
Overweight management
  • Calorie deficit (500 kcal below TDEE)
  • Avoid refined sugars, saturated fat, processed foods
  • Increase physical activity; behavioral counselling
  • BMI >25 = overweight; >30 = obese (WHO)
III

Infant & Child Nutrition

Feeding · Weaning · Sick child · School nutrition

▾
👶
Feeding Normal Infants

tap to expand

Nutritional requirements (0–12 months)
  • Energy: ~100–120 kcal/kg/day (high due to rapid growth)
  • Protein: 1.5–2.2 g/kg/day
  • Fat: 40–50% of calories; DHA for brain development
  • Iron: needs supplementation after 6 months
  • Calcium: 200–260 mg/day (0–6m), 260–270 mg/day (6–12m)
Breastfeeding
  • WHO recommends exclusive breastfeeding for 6 months
  • Colostrum (first milk) – rich in antibodies (IgA), proteins, vitamins
  • Breast milk ideal composition changes over time
  • Advantages: immunity, bonding, cost-effective, reduces SIDS
Formula feeding (when breastfeeding not possible)
  • Iron-fortified infant formula
  • Do NOT use cow's milk <12 months (kidney load, low iron)
🍼
Feeding Premature Infants

tap to expand

Factors to consider
  • Gestational age and birth weight (VLBW <1500g; LBW <2500g)
  • Weak suck/swallow reflex – may need gavage (tube) feeding
  • Immature gut – risk of NEC (Necrotising Enterocolitis)
  • Higher metabolic rate; rapid catch-up growth needed
Nutritional requirements (higher than term infants)
  • Energy: 110–135 kcal/kg/day
  • Protein: 3–4 g/kg/day (for catch-up growth)
  • Calcium & phosphorus: higher for bone mineralisation
  • Vitamins D, A, C, E, K supplementation required
  • Iron supplementation from 2–4 weeks of age
  • Expressed breast milk is gold standard; fortification often needed
🥣
Supplementary Feeding & Weaning

tap to expand

Supplementary feeding advantages
  • Fills energy-nutrient gap as breast milk alone becomes insufficient after 6 months
  • Provides iron, zinc, B12 not adequately in breast milk after 6m
  • Develops chewing skills and food acceptance
Method of introduction
  • Start at 6 months; introduce ONE food at a time, 3–5 day gap
  • Begin with rice/dal kanji, mashed vegetables, fruit purees
  • Increase texture gradually: puree → mashed → chopped → family food
  • No added salt/sugar/honey under 1 year
Weaning – effects on mother and child
  • Mother: menstrual cycle returns, risk of pregnancy increases, breast engorgement initially
  • Child: shift from passive (milk) to active (solid) nutrition; risk of infections if done abruptly or unhygienically
🧠
Psychology of Infant & Child Feeding

tap to expand

  • Feeding is a social/emotional experience as well as nutritional
  • Responsive feeding – respond to hunger/satiety cues; avoid force feeding
  • Food neophobia – common in toddlers; offer rejected foods 8–15 times
  • Avoid using food as reward or punishment
  • Positive mealtime environment – family meals, minimal distractions
  • Parent's anxiety can transfer to child's eating behaviour
  • Media influence on food choices in older children
🤒
Feeding the Sick Child & Diseases in Childhood

tap to expand

General principles
  • Continue breastfeeding during illness
  • Small frequent meals; easy-to-digest foods
  • Adequate fluids/ORS to prevent dehydration
  • Catch-up feeding after recovery
Common conditions
  • Diarrhoea – ORS, continue feeding, zinc supplementation
  • Fever – extra 10–12% energy per °C rise; fluids
  • Coeliac disease – strict gluten-free diet for life
  • PKU (Phenylketonuria) – low phenylalanine formula
  • Type 1 Diabetes – carb counting, insulin coordination
Deficiency states in children
  • Marasmus – severe wasting; sunken eyes, skin and bones
  • Kwashiorkor – oedema, moon face, pot belly, hair discolouration
  • Vitamin A deficiency – bitot's spots, xerophthalmia
  • Rickets – bowed legs, delayed dentition; Vit D deficiency
  • Iron deficiency anaemia – pallor, poor concentration, fatigue
🧒
Pre-school Child Nutrition (1–6 yrs)

tap to expand

Nutritional needs
  • Energy: 1000–1400 kcal/day (varies by age)
  • Protein: 1.1–1.5 g/kg/day
  • Iron: 7–10 mg/day; calcium: 700–1000 mg/day
  • Vitamin A, D, C from variety of foods
Factors in diet planning
  • Small stomach capacity – small frequent meals (5–6/day)
  • Food preferences – involve child in choices
  • Texture, colour, presentation matter
  • Avoid choking hazards (whole nuts, hard raw vegetables)
Common feeding problems
  • Food jags – eating only one food repeatedly
  • Poor appetite during slower growth phase (toddler period)
  • Dental caries from sticky sweet foods
🏫
School Lunch Programme

tap to expand

Advantages
  • Ensures at least one balanced meal/day for school children
  • Improves attendance and enrolment (especially girls)
  • Reduces hunger and improves concentration and learning
  • Reduces nutritional deficiencies in vulnerable children
  • Promotes healthy food habits from early age
Need in India
  • High prevalence of malnutrition and anaemia in school children
  • Mid-Day Meal Scheme (MDMS) – launched 1995, covers >120 million children
  • Provides 450 kcal + 12g protein to primary school children
  • Reduces dropout rate, especially in rural areas
  • PM POSHAN Shakti Nirman (renamed 2021)

Community Nutrition

Special groups · Assessment · National programmes

🌍
Community Nutrition Programmes

tap to expand

Need for community nutrition programmes
  • High prevalence of malnutrition, hidden hunger (micronutrient deficiency)
  • Vulnerability of specific groups – pregnant women, infants, elderly
  • Link between nutrition, productivity and development (SDGs)
  • Need to bridge gap between dietary intake and requirements
Key national programmes (India)
  • ICDS – Integrated Child Development Services (supplementary nutrition + health)
  • PM POSHAN (Mid-Day Meal) – school children
  • POSHAN Abhiyaan (National Nutrition Mission) – 2018
  • National Iron + Initiative – IDA in children & adolescents
  • Vitamin A Supplementation Programme
  • Iodine Deficiency Disorder (IDD) Control – universal salt iodisation
  • RMNCH+A – reproductive, maternal, newborn & child health
🤰
Nutritional Needs – Special Groups

tap to expand

Pregnant women
  • +350 kcal/day (2nd trimester); +450 kcal (3rd trimester)
  • Protein: +23 g/day above normal requirement
  • Iron: 35 mg/day (ICMR); folate: 500 μg/day (prevent NTDs)
  • Calcium: 1200 mg/day; Iodine critical for fetal brain
Lactating mothers
  • +500–600 kcal/day above normal
  • High fluid intake (2.5–3 litres); calcium 1200 mg/day
  • Protein +25 g/day; Vitamin A 950 μg RE/day
Adolescents
  • Rapid growth spurt – high energy, protein, calcium, iron needs
  • Girls: iron-rich diet for menstrual losses
  • Zinc critical for sexual maturation
Elderly (>60 years)
  • Reduced BMR – lower calorie needs
  • Adequate protein to prevent sarcopenia
  • Calcium + Vit D – prevent osteoporosis
  • High fibre – constipation prevention; B12 – absorption decreases with age
🥜
Cheap, Nutritious & Vegetarian Substitutes

tap to expand

Substitutes for non-vegetarian foods
  • Protein: pulses, legumes, soya, paneer, milk, tofu, groundnuts
  • Iron: dark leafy greens (palak, methi), bajra, jaggery, fortified foods
  • Vitamin B12: dairy, fortified cereals (challenging in pure vegan)
  • Omega-3: flaxseeds, walnuts, chia seeds
  • Zinc: pumpkin seeds, whole grains, legumes
Cheap and nutritious food selection
  • Millets (bajra, jowar, ragi) – affordable, nutrient-dense
  • Seasonal local vegetables & fruits
  • Fermented foods – increase bioavailability (idli, dosa, curd)
  • Germinated/sprouted pulses – enhance iron & vitamin C
📚
Nutrition Education

tap to expand

Need
  • Improve dietary behaviours and food choices
  • Reduce nutrition-related diseases (PEM, NCDs)
  • Empower communities to achieve food security
Methods
  • Individual counselling – diet history, personalised advice
  • Group talks – schools, PHCs, ASHA workers
  • Demonstrations – cooking, food preparation
  • Mass media – TV, radio, social media campaigns
  • Printed materials – posters, pamphlets, flip charts
  • Community kitchen gardens – promote local fresh produce
  • School-based nutrition education programmes
📊
Assessing Nutritional Status

tap to expand

ABCD Method
  • A – Anthropometric: Weight, height, BMI, MUAC, head circumference, WAZ/HAZ/WHZ in children
  • B – Biochemical: Haemoglobin, serum albumin, serum ferritin, vitamin levels, urine creatinine
  • C – Clinical: Physical signs – pallor, oedema, bitot's spots, glossitis, goitre, skin changes
  • D – Dietary: 24-hour recall, food frequency questionnaire (FFQ), food diary, dietary history
Community/Group level
  • Surveys – NFHS (National Family Health Survey), NNS
  • Vital statistics – infant mortality rate, under-5 mortality
  • Ecological assessment – food supply, socioeconomic data
  • WHO growth standards for children
🚨
Current Nutritional Problems in India

tap to expand

Undernutrition
  • Stunting – 35.5% children under 5 (NFHS-5, 2019–21)
  • Wasting – 19.3%; Underweight – 32.1%
  • Anaemia – 67% children (6–59m), 57% women of reproductive age
Overnutrition / Double burden
  • Rising obesity & overweight – especially urban populations
  • Diabetes, hypertension, CVD increasing – diet-related NCDs
Micronutrient deficiencies ("Hidden hunger")
  • Iron, Vitamin A, Iodine, Zinc, Vitamin D deficiencies widespread
National response
  • POSHAN Abhiyaan 2.0 – targets stunting, wasting, anaemia, LBW
  • Fortification of staples (rice, wheat flour, salt with iron/folic acid/B12)
  • ICDS, NHM, SBM for sanitation (affects nutrition outcomes)
